Abstract—Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) play a 
vital role in supporting Indonesia’s economic growth. However, 
their adoption of Artificial Intelligence (AI) remains limited 
despite its potential to enhance efficiency, innovation, and 
competitiveness. This study extends the Technology Acceptance 
Model (TAM) to explore how perceived ease of use, perceived 
usefulness, and perceived security influence SMEs’ intention to 
adopt AI technologies. A quantitative approach was employed 
using survey data collected from 131 Indonesian SME owners 
and managers. The data were analyzed using Partial Least 
Squares–Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM). The 
findings reveal that perceived ease of use significantly shapes 
both the perceived usefulness of AI and the intention to adopt it, 
consistent with TAM's theoretical propositions. Interestingly, 
perceived usefulness does not affect substantially adoption 
intention, suggesting that functional benefits alone are 
insufficient to drive adoption in this context. Instead, perceived 
security emerges as a critical determinant, indicating that SMEs 
place strong emphasis on data protection, privacy, and system 
reliability when considering AI implementation. This study 
contributes to refining the TAM framework by highlighting the 
central role of security as a determinant of adoption behavior 
and demonstrating the limited influence of perceived usefulness 
in high-risk technological settings. The results underscore the 
need for policymakers and technology providers to prioritize 
secure and accessible AI solutions to foster inclusive and 
sustainable digital transformation among Indonesian SMEs. 
Keywords—Artificial Intelligence; Technology Acceptance 
Model; Security; Perceived Usefulness; Perceived Ease of Use 
I. INTRODUCTION  
Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) are essential to 
Indonesia's economic growth, as they employ the bulk of the 
country's workforce and account for more than 60% of its 
GDP [1]. Artificial Intelligence (AI) has become a key 
technology that may improve competitiveness, efficiency, and 
decision-making in the age of rapid digital transformation. 
Adopting AI is now essential for Indonesian SMEs to prosper 
in increasingly globalized and dynamic marketplaces, not just 
a luxury [2]. 
However, Indonesian SMEs continue to deploy AI 
technologies unevenly, with several obstacles. Technology 
acceptability, which includes managers' and entrepreneurs' 
readiness to incorporate AI technologies into their business 
operations, is one of the most critical elements affecting 
adoption [3]. Perceptions of usability, compatibility with 
current systems, and ease of use influence this adoption. 
Security is also critical because worries about data privacy, 
cyber risks, and regulatory compliance greatly impact trust 
and the desire to use AI breakthroughs [4]. 
An unusual setting for studying these processes is 
Indonesia. Government programs like Making Indonesia 4.0 
and the acceleration of the development of the digital 
economy are driving the nation's rapid increases in internet 
adoption and digital literacy [5]. SMEs' limitations may 
hamper the implementation of AI in infrastructure, 
knowledge, and resources. Therefore, creating strategies that 
help SMEs leverage AI for sustained growth requires an 
understanding of how security perceptions and technology 
acceptability influence AI adoption behavior. 
Recent scholarship on AI and technology adoption in 
SMEs has identified multiple factors shaping successful 
implementation. Within the Technology–Organization–
Environment (TOE) framework [6], drivers such as relative 
advantage, compatibility, human capital, market demand, and 
government support emerge as critical, with evidence 
suggesting that medium-sized firms benefit more strongly 
from relative advantage than smaller firms [7]. Similarly, 
research using the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of 
Technology (UTAUT) model shows that effort expectancy 
and social influence significantly affect behavioral intention 
to adopt generative AI, with age and work experience 
moderating these effects [8]. Leadership has also been shown 
to influence AI adoption through fostering data-driven 
cultures and enhancing employee competencies, which in turn 
strengthen supply chain resilience [9]. Yet, in many 
developing country contexts, SMEs continue to rely primarily 
on basic information systems rather than advanced 
technologies such as AI, underscoring the importance of 
sociocultural and contextualized adoption models [10]. 
Building upon the preceding discussion, this study offers 
a novel contribution by examining technology acceptance and 
security perceptions concurrently as determinants of AI 
adoption intention among Indonesian SMEs, a sector 
experiencing rapid digital transformation yet marked by 
uneven technological uptake. The Technology Acceptance 
Model (TAM) serves as the central theoretical framework for 
elucidating user behavior toward emerging technologies, 
providing a structured lens for understanding the underlying 
drivers of AI adoption. 
In this study, AI technologies refer to practical, business-
oriented applications that support SMEs’ operational and 
strategic activities. In Indonesia, SMEs have increasingly 
adopted various forms of AI, including AI-driven customer

In contrast to earlier research that predominantly focuses 
on organizational, environmental, or leadership dimensions, 
this study emphasizes three key constructs: perceived ease of 
use, perceived usefulness, and perceived security. These 
dimensions capture the practical realities and concerns of 
SMEs operating in resource-constrained and high-risk

approach, the research not only advances the existing TAM 
framework by integrating security as a critical factor but also 
provides context-specific evidence on how Indonesian SMEs 
assess the opportunities and challenges of AI implementation. 
Ultimately, this study seeks to illuminate how perceptions of 
usability, utility, and security interact to shape adoption 
behavior, offering actionable insights for policymakers, 
technology developers, and SME practitioners while 
contributing to the broader theoretical discourse on 
technology acceptance and digital transformation. 
Despite growing recognition of AI’s transformative 
potential, the mechanisms through which technology 
acceptance and security shape adoption behavior among 
Indonesian SMEs remain insufficiently explored. This gap 
limits policymakers' and practitioners' ability to design 
effective strategies to address SMEs’ specific barriers to 
digital transformation. Accordingly, this paper is structured as 
follows: Section II reviews the theoretical foundation and 
relevant literature; Section III outlines the research 
methodology; Section IV presents and discusses the empirical 
findings; and Section V concludes with implications, 
limitations, and directions for future research. 
II. LITERATURE REVIEW 
Grounded in the constructs of perceived usefulness and 
perceived ease of use, the Technology Acceptance Model 
(TAM) provides a structured framework for understanding 
how individuals and organizations form intentions to engage 
with emerging technologies. When integrated with the Theory 
of Planned Behavior, TAM has demonstrated strong 
explanatory power, accounting for approximately 59% of the 
variance in AI acceptance across various contexts [11]. Its 
adaptability has been evident in multiple domains. For 
instance, in the hospitality and tourism industry, the 
development of an interactive TAM (iTAM) highlights the 
critical role of perceived interactivity in shaping user attitudes 
toward AI-enabled service robots [12]. Similarly, studies 
combining TAM with status quo bias theory in the context of 
AI voice assistants show that perceived ease of use and 
perceived value positively influence user attitudes, whereas 
switching costs and perceived risks act as barriers to adoption 
[13]. In the education sector, TAM has been employed to 
examine teachers’ intentions to utilize AI-based e-learning 
platforms, revealing that perceptions of usefulness and 
emotional 
engagement 
significantly 
impact 
adoption 
decisions [14]. Collectively, these findings underscore TAM’s 
relevance as a theoretical lens for exploring how perceptions 
of utility, usability, and security interact to shape AI adoption 
behaviors—particularly in dynamic, resource-constrained 
environments such as SMEs. 
Studies investigating the impact of Perceived Ease of Use 
(PEU) on AI adoption intentions (IAI) demonstrate 
consistently favorable correlations across several areas. In 
accounting education, PEU serves as a mediator between 
technological preparedness and aspirations to use AI, in 
conjunction with perceived utility [15]. Likewise, PEU has 
substantial positive effects on both the desire to use and the 
actual adoption of digital technologies, such as e-wallets [16]. 
In the realm of AI-enabled smart speakers, PEU is a pivotal 
aspect impacting attitudes and intentions for continued usage, 
whereas social elements such as media richness and parasocial 
interactions shape perceptions of PEU [17]. These results 
repeatedly validate PEU as an essential determinant in AI 
adoption across different contexts and user demographics. 
[18] discovered that in small and medium firms in Taiwan, 
only perceived ease of use (PEU) had a direct impact on 
perceived usefulness (PU), and PU was the only direct 
predictor of system utilization. This is different from the 
original TAM research by [19], which revealed that PU had a 
significant effect on user intentions, whereas PEU had a 
weaker but still significant effect that weakened over time. 
The positive correlation between PEU and PU has been 
consistently validated across several areas. [20] validated that 
PEU substantially influences PU in the context of e-book 
uptake among university students. [15] also discovered that 
both PEU and PU act as intermediaries in the interaction 
between technological readiness and the adoption of artificial 
intelligence in accounting contexts, demonstrating their 
ongoing significance in contemporary technology acceptance 
frameworks. 
PU acts as a bridge between students' readiness to use 
technology and their plans to use AI [15]. In the same way, 
studies of employees demonstrate that PU completely 
mediates the negative link between job instability and plans to 
use AI, and somewhat mediates the positive link between 
habit and plans to utilize AI at work [21]. PU affects more than 
only AI-specific applications. For example, research on the 
adoption of e-wallets demonstrates that PU has a significant 
beneficial impact on both usage intentions and actual adoption 
behaviors [16]. In AI assistant-enabled smart speakers, 
perceived usefulness (PU) is a significant factor that affects 
how users feel about the technology and whether they plan to 
keep using it. It works with the perceived simplicity of use to 
encourage people to adopt the technology [22]. These results 
consistently affirm PU as a critical factor in technology 
adoption decisions. 
In the realm of financial services, technical optimism 
enhances the intention to utilize AI-driven robo-advisors, but 
insecurity diminishes adoption intentions; conversely, 
technological discomfort unexpectedly had good outcomes 
[23]. In terms of security issues, insecurity makes people think 
that AI systems are not very beneficial, especially in retail 
settings, where being inventive and optimistic makes both 
PEOU and PU go up [24]. These findings indicate that security 
concerns and feelings of unease consistently act as obstacles 
to AI adoption across diverse fields.

Abstract— Food consumption is increasing significantly on global 
stage due to social and economic growth, leading to higher food 
production and development in food supply chain (FSC). Many 
firms have been adopting artificial intelligence (AI) in FSC to deal 
with problems. This research aims to investigate the influence of 
AI technology adoption of micro, small, and medium enterprise 
(MSME) FSC in aspect of traceability and transparency in 
Thailand. As various statistical research discussed in the 
introduction section points out that nowadays FSC still lack of 
traceability and transparency, which affects to product 
consumption of customers. A quantitative approach through an 
online questionnaire is utilized to gather statistical data, along 
with conducting the literature review from existing literatures. 92 
of MSMEs in Thailand are used as a sample for this research then 
linear regression is applied for data analysis. The result of the 
study indicates that AI significantly influences firm’s performance 
in term of traceability and transparency. AI facilitates in sourcing, 
production, scheduling, and transportation. It provides a better 
decision making and ensures that all processes in FSC reach 
standards. However, there are some limitations in technology 
adoption for MSMEs specifically in micro businesses as they still 
lack know-how and budget for technological investment. 
Index Terms—Food supply chain, artificial intelligence, 
traceability, transparency, MSME 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Over 8.1 billion people at present globally, food production 
and consumption have become way more significant issue 
comparing to the past. The main goal of the food industry is to 
provide good foods and other necessities that is suitable for the 
diversity of human’s activities and lifestyle. In order to serve 
the expanding population, the method of interconnected 
activities that operate in the food supply chain (FSC) must be 
transformed significantly [1]. FSC refers to all complex 
networks between farm and processing activities, distributors, 
and retailers that operate the concept of ‘farm to fork’ [2]. The 
process begins by the raw material until final products are 
delivered to customers. [2] employed a systematic review on 
challenges in FSC. The result indicates the three most common 
issues in FSC, including sustainability, food safety and quality, 
traceability, and transparency. Among three issues, traceability 
and transparency gains the lowest percentage of distribution in 
term of being mentioned by articles [2], implying that 
traceability and transparency still do not gain much attention in 
majority comparing to other two issues, although it is an 
important topic. FSC with a lack of traceability and 
transparency will adversely impact the efficiency on FSC [3]. 
Nowadays food trade becomes globalized, food chain integrity 
is not concerning only about food safety but also with its origin 
[4]. In most region, most academic research in firm’s 
performance highlighted more on large firms rather than a small 
and medium firm [5]. Although MSMEs are a core economic 
driver in many countries, but still become neglected compared 
to large firm. Particularly in Thailand, MSMEs play a vital role 
in the Thai economy contributing to 35.2% of the total gross 
domestic product (GDP). MSMEs generated GDP value as 
amounting to 1,784,473 million baht, representing 39.97% of 
the GDP in manufacturing sector as a whole [6]. These 
statistical data implies that Thai MSMEs is an important part in 
Thai economy and should be gained more attention from many 
departments. 
      Nowadays traceability and transparency issue, tracking and 
tracing technology is still not widely standardized. Outdated 
technologies and traditional manual methods can cause a lack of 
traceability. [7] had addressed some barriers to AI adoption in 
FSC. The challenge for AI adoption is due to lacking an 
appropriate testing system or trial facilities of AI technology, 
especially in the scope of our study, MSMEs. Local MSMEs still 
do not have the readiness to become fully invested in 
technology. Therefore, this research aims to examine the 
influence of AI technology adoption in FSC on MSMEs’ 
business performance in aspect of traceability and transparency, 
along with studying the gap that may occur for the adoption.

B. Food Traceability and Transparency 
[12] defined food traceability as part of logistic 
management which captures, stores, and transfers information 
about food in the entire stages of FSC so that products can be 
verified to control quality and safety, tracked forwardly, and 
traced backwardly at any time required. Three main processes 
are required for a tracking system in FSC, including the 
transformation of analog data to digital data, transmission of 
digital data from a local source to the database, lastly the user 
assess and collaboration [13]. Transparency refers to the 
disclosure of a product’s information, such as production 
process, ingredients, environmental and social impacts, 
certification, label that indicates the product’s quality and so on 
[14]. [13] mentioned that the major cause of food 
contamination is from lack of traceability and transparency. As 
food takes a long path from producer to customer, food 
contamination can occur easily at any stage of FSC leading to a 
negative domino effect. Once it happens, it takes a long time to 
point out the exact source and reason of contamination and 
specifically identify the contaminated products. This issue can 
become a crucial concern to all members in FSC, especially 
those who are still lacking digital and technology skills [4]. 
C. Artificial Intelligence  
Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a sub-field of computer science 
involving with creation of innovative machines and computer 
systems that can perform any task which requires human 
intelligence [15]. These innovations have the potential to 
directly impact both the manufacturing processes and the details 
of several products and services, with important implications for 
productivity, employment, and competition [16].  [17] employed 
a systematic review of AI applications in supply chain and 
classified AI according to their techniques into four sub-fields. 
First sub-field includes Artificial neural network and Rough set 
theory. The second sub-field includes Machine learning, Expert 
systems and Genetic algorithms. The third sub-field includes 
Fuzzy logic. Lastly, forth sub-field includes Agent-based 
systems. This study is going to highlight on the outstanding AI 
techniques based on the result of application in the supply chain 
and adapting to FSC for this study. According to the result from 
[17], machine learning is the most dominant type of AI that has 
been used throughout all processes in supply chain. Genetic 
algorithm is the second most used type of AI which is used in 
five out of six processes in supply chain. Artificial neural 
network, Expert system, Fuzzy logic, and Agent-based system 
are applied in three processes. However, only ANN is engaged 
in purchasing and procurement, production, and inventory 
management which are core processes that directly affect the 
product’s quality and major management in all supply chain. 
Therefore, this study will highlight three potential AI sub-fields, 
including machine learning, genetic algorithm, and artificial 
neural network 
a) Machine Learning: Machine learning (ML) is a subset 
of AI that facilitates the system to be capable of identifying 
patterns and making decision by learning and improving from 
experience and previous data set without being programmed 
[18]. Algorithms are trained using a large collection of two data 
groups, the training set and the testing set. The algorithm will 
receive a set of inputs with correct outputs, known as pre-
labeled information, to compare its actual output with correct 
outputs and spot errors [19]. Regression is an example of 
machine learning that will be applied to this research. 
Regression is used to determine a relationship between the 
independent variable and the dependent variable. It instructs a 
model using a dataset of X and Y and uses the trained model to 
predict the output of new input data [26].  
b) Genetic Algorithm: Genetic algorithm (GA) refers to 
the principle of natural selection and agorithms inspired by the 
biological evolution of living beings [20]. The fittest 
chromosome will survive to generate new offspring, and this 
process is replicated for multiple generations [21]. The weaker 
chromosome will be eliminated. This technique is used for 
optimization problems by finding the maximum or minimum 
value. [22]. There are five components in GA, which are 
chromosome representation, selection, crossover, mutation, and 
fitness function computation [23]. Selection, crossover, and 
mutation are considered as three core operations in GA. 
Selection is where a chromosome in the population will be in a 
structure that hold information related to a potential solution 
with a combination of weights [24]. Crossover is to combine 
chromosomes of two parent to produce new offspring that is 
phenotypically like the parents [25]. Mutation is proformed 
intentionally to stop falling of all solution in the population to 
an optimal level of the solved problem [21]. 
c) Artificial Neural Network: Artificial neural network 
(ANN) is a processing technique that imitates the working of 
brain cell and the nervous system of humans. Its main goal is to 
figure out the pattern in dataset and do the clustering according 
to natural grouping in the data [26]. The neuron, a special 
component of an ANN, plays an important role in imitating the 
human brain to perform tasks. Neurons communicate by 
transmitting electrical and chemical signal to neighbors through 
a neuron’s branch called an axon, which is further segmented 
to collaterals [27]. Neurons transfers signals to the receiving 
end by the formation of connections between synapses, 
neuromuscular junctions at the end of collaterals, and 
neighboring neurons. At the receicing end, neuron’s dendrites 
receive signals via synapses and merge them with neuron’s 
body called the soma. Neurons with stronger synaptic

Abstract— In the context of today's rapidly evolving technological
landscape and the urbanization of Tier 1 and Tier 2 cities, cloud
kitchens have emerged as pivotal players, offering quick and
convenient meals without the need for dine-in facilities. The
demand prediction for the food has become quite tough and could
be considered as one of the significant challenges. Cloud kitchens
face challenges in managing stock due to the absence of in-house
diners, potentially resulting in wasted food. Moreover, adverse
weather conditions often hinder the seamless delivery of online.
To address these challenges, this project employs machine
learning techniques to predict weather patterns and correlate them
with food preferences according to that weather. The key
algorithms found to deal with this problem were Random Forest,
kNN, Decision tree and Naïve Bayes and Support Vector Machine.
This serves the first half of the statement and then for
recommendation a suitable system was developed. The pivotal role
of the system is to reduce the food wastages for cloud kitchens by
fostering adaptive strategies for efficient decision making. The key
inputs from the market were found to be that 30-40% of the
predicted sales in food industry doesn’t meet due to various
reasons. The ultimate goal is to empower cloud kitchen operations,
ensuring adaptive and informed decision-making in response to
changing weather dynamics. The union of both of these are
explained using Interpretability tool SHAP for a convenient study. 
Keywords: Cloud kitchens, Decision Tree, Random Forest,
Recommendation system, SHAP, Weather pattern 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Weather predictions serve as a critical determinant in
various sectors, playing an indispensable role in strategic
decision-making, planning, and risk management. For the
food industry, especially cloud kitchens and restaurants,
understanding and predicting weather patterns is top.
Unforeseen weather fluctuations significantly impact
customer behavior and preferences, leading to variations in
demand and sales. Predicting the weather can help businesses
plan better for changes in customer demand and manage their
resources efficiently. By using advanced weather forecasts
and smart computer programs, companies can adjust their
plans based on expected weather forecasts. This helps them
avoid problems caused by sudden shifts in customer behavior
due to weather changes. This active approach is especially
helpful for cloud kitchens and restaurants. It allows them to
organize their work better, use their resources wisely, and
reduce food waste caused by unpredictable weather. With
technology getting better, there are different ways to predict
the weather. The study in this article explores at various
methods to find the most accurate and efficient ones. 
 
Throwing away food isn't just about losing meals and
wasting it. It also means losing a lot of money for businesses
and causing big problems for the environment. Businesses
lose money when they can't sell the food they've prepared,
and it costs money to get rid of the extra food. Plus, making
food uses up a lot of resources like water and energy, and
when food gets wasted, it's like wasting all those resources,
too. Cloud kitchens, which are becoming more popular, have
a hard time managing how much food they need and reducing 
waste. Because they don't have physical customers coming
in, things like bad weather affecting food deliveries can make
it even harder for them. According to a report by Food and
Agriculture Organization (FAO), for each food produced, one
third of it goes to mere waste which eventually challenges the
sustainability of the planet. 
 
When weather forecasts and food businesses work
together, it can really help tackle problems. For cloud
kitchens, using these weather predictions in their planning
can make a big difference. When they use smart systems that
learn from the weather forecasts, they can make food better 
with less waste and adjust their plans. This integration 
between weather predictions and food businesses helps these
kitchens work better, matching the changing weather and
what customers want, making things more efficient and eco-
friendly.
